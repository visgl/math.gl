Understanding myriahedral projections

A myriahedral map replaces one continuous world map with many small, connected panels. Approximate the globe with a fine polyhedron, decide which edges remain hinged, cut the others, and unfold the panels into a plane. The gaps are part of the projection rather than rendering errors.

Try the interactive example, or use the reusable API to project your own geographic data without an animation or WebGL renderer.

Origins and credits​

Jarke J. van Wijk introduced this approach in Unfolding the Earth: Myriahedral Projections, The Cartographic Journal 45(1), 32–42 (2008). The paper studies graticule meshes, recursively subdivided polyhedra, and meshes derived from geography. See the publication record and abstract and paper DOI.

Nicolas “Nico” Belmonte's interactive work is the inspiration for this example. His February 2018 vis.gl article, Unfolding the Earth: Myriahedral Projections In WebGL, celebrates the research's tenth anniversary. His WebGL demonstration makes the relationship between a globe, cuts, and a planar map tangible.

The article also describes geography-aligned meshes built from distance fields and streamlines. That is a different construction from assigning geography-based weights to a fixed graticule. Our sealine and coastline presets use the latter; they do not reproduce his coastline-derived mesh topology.

math.gl's mesh generation, edge weights, panel charts, and unfolding are independently implemented. We have not copied Nico's source code, mesh tables, textures, animation frames, or optimized solid orientations. Credit identifies the conceptual and visual inspiration; it does not imply a license to his assets.

What the cuts buy you​

A rigid flat panel can rotate without stretching. A curved patch of Earth cannot be flattened with all distances, areas, and angles preserved. Those are different steps: our radial projection onto a finite panel has local distortion; unfolding that panel adds none. Smaller panels improve the approximation, at the cost of more boundaries and gaps. The implementation makes no exact conformal or equal-area guarantee.

This is useful for explaining projection tradeoffs, exploring alternative world layouts, and displaying geography on articulated surfaces. A conventional map may be easier to read when following a route across many interruptions. The right layout depends on what needs to remain visually connected.

A mesh and a tree​

Imagine one graph node for each panel and an edge for each neighboring pair. Assign a high weight to a join worth keeping. A maximum spanning tree retains connections that reach every panel without forming cycles. The remaining mesh edges become cuts. This gives a mechanically consistent net, but says nothing about whether separate branches overlap after unfolding.

For a fixed mesh and weights, the spanning-tree step maximizes the total retained weight. It does not optimize the sphere's orientation, redesign the mesh, search all possible world layouts, or guarantee that a continent remains whole. The example reports overlaps so a visually plausible arrangement is not mistaken for a globally valid, single-valued flat map.

Choosing a family​

FamilyWhat changesmath.gl support
GraticuleDirection and location of joins on a longitude/latitude meshPolyconic, cylindric, azimuthal, hemispheres, sealine, coastline
PolyhedralBase solid and refinement levelAll five Platonic solids, including recursive triangle refinement
Geography weightedWhich fixed-mesh joins surviveApplication-supplied land mask and custom edge weights
Geography alignedThe mesh itself follows geographic structureCustom mesh input; no built-in distance-field/streamline generator

The names describe the appearance and join strategy. A myriahedral polyconic net is not the analytic polyconic CRS projection. Likewise, a land-weighted icosahedron is not automatically the Dymaxion or Gray–Fuller projection.

At refinement level zero, polyhedral presets keep the solid's original polygonal faces. For higher levels, polygon faces are triangulated around their spherical centers, then triangles are repeatedly split using shared, normalized edge midpoints. This is our construction; exact facet counts and arrangements need not match another implementation.

Using cuts correctly in an application​

A longitude/latitude can occur on several panel boundaries. A flat coordinate can also lie inside multiple overlapping panels. Keep the panel ID with projected geometry and with picks. Render clipped line and polygon pieces separately; connecting pieces across a cut creates false bridges.

The static API returns all valid inverse candidates rather than choosing a potentially wrong continent. The animated example picks the visible face and uses its original spherical coordinates. Both views use the same panel order, so applications can transition between flat geometry and rigid unfolding.

The example's land colors use public-domain Natural Earth data. NASA Blue Marble is an optional attributed texture. Those sources are independent of the projection algorithm and of Nico's demonstration; see the example's imagery and data provenance.
