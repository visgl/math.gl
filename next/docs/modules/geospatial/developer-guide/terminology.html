Geospatial terminology

Geospatial code combines several kinds of geometry: coordinates in a reference system, paths on a curved surface, straight lines in three-dimensional space, and projected shapes on a map. This guide defines the terms used throughout math.gl and distinguishes concepts that can look similar in a rendering.

Start with geodesic, rhumb line, chord, and ellipsoid. For the mathematical construction of map families, see map projection families.

Earth and surface models​

Sphere​

A surface whose points are all the same distance R from its center. A spherical Earth model is useful for visualization and many world-map formulas, but its distances and areas differ from those on an ellipsoid. A radius is part of the model, not an interchangeable choice of units.

Ellipsoid and spheroid​

An ellipsoid satisfies x²/a² + y²/b² + z²/c² = 1, where a, b, and c are its semiaxes. A spheroid, or ellipsoid of revolution, has two equal semiaxes. Earth reference ellipsoids are usually oblate spheroids: the equatorial radius is larger than the polar radius. A triaxial ellipsoid can have three different semiaxes. An ellipsoid models a smooth reference surface, not terrain or sea level.

For an oblate spheroid, a commonly denotes the equatorial semimajor axis and b the polar semiminor axis. Flattening is f = (a − b) / a; first eccentricity squared is e² = f(2 − f). Inverse flattening is 1/f, when f is nonzero.

See Ellipsoid for geometry and conversions.

Geoid and terrain​

The geoid is a gravity equipotential surface used as a reference for physical heights and associated with mean sea level. It is irregular relative to an ellipsoid. Terrain is the actual ground surface; a digital elevation model (DEM) samples some specified surface and height reference. A DEM is not necessarily ellipsoidal, and a geoid model is not a terrain model.

Normal and tangent plane​

A surface normal is perpendicular to the surface at a point. On a sphere it is radial; on an ellipsoid it generally does not point through the center. A tangent plane passes through the point and is perpendicular to its normal. It approximates the surface locally; distances in that plane are not generally surface distances over a large region.

Coordinates and reference systems​

TermMeaning
LongitudeAngular position around the polar axis, measured from a prime meridian. It becomes indeterminate at a pole.
Geodetic latitudeAngle between the ellipsoid's outward normal and the equatorial plane. Geographic ellipsoid coordinates normally use this latitude.
Geocentric latitudeAngle between the center-to-point direction and the equatorial plane. It generally differs from geodetic latitude on an ellipsoid.
MeridianA constant-longitude curve connecting the poles. The prime meridian defines longitude zero.
ParallelA constant-latitude curve. On a sphere, only the equator is also a great circle.
AntimeridianThe meridian opposite a chosen prime or central meridian. ±180° are two common representations of the same longitude relative to Greenwich.
Antipodal pointsOpposite points through the center of a sphere or ellipsoid. On a sphere, antipodal endpoints admit infinitely many shortest great-circle routes.
ECEFEarth-centered, Earth-fixed Cartesian coordinates. The axes rotate with Earth; positions are commonly expressed in metres.
ENUA local east, north, up frame. Its origin and definition of up must be specified; ellipsoidal up follows the surface normal.
CRSA coordinate reference system: a coordinate system related to Earth through a datum or reference frame. Its axis order, units, and height interpretation matter.
Datum / reference frameThe reference that anchors coordinates to Earth. An ellipsoid alone does not specify the position, orientation, realization, or time dependence of a geodetic frame.
WGS84A geodetic reference system with an associated ellipsoid and reference-frame realizations. “WGS84 ellipsoid” specifies a surface shape; it does not specify every detail of a coordinate reference system.
Coordinate epochThe time at which coordinates represent a position in a time-dependent frame. It can differ from the frame's reference epoch.
Geographic / projected / geocentric CRSRespectively, angular coordinates on a reference surface, planar coordinates from a map projection, and three-dimensional Earth-centered coordinates.
EPSG identifierA registry identifier for a CRS or other geodetic object. It is not a universal declaration of array order or a substitute for inspecting the object's definition.

math.gl's geographic convenience APIs generally use longitude first, angles in degrees, and ellipsoidal height in metres. A CRS-defined axis order can differ. Check the particular API rather than assuming every three-number array means the same thing. See coordinate reference systems.

Heights and vertical references​

TermMeaning
Ellipsoidal height, hHeight relative to a reference ellipsoid, measured along its normal. It is not automatically height above sea level.
Orthometric height, HA gravity-based height referenced to the geoid, associated with distance along the plumb line. Commonly used for physical elevations.
Geoid height / undulation, NSeparation between a geoid model and its reference ellipsoid. With compatible references, the usual height-conversion relation is h ≈ H + N.
Vertical datumThe reference defining a height system. Two datasets labeled “elevation in metres” may use different vertical datums.
Altitude / elevationContext-dependent names for height. Specify the surface, datum, and units instead of relying on the name alone.

A height conversion requires compatible horizontal and vertical references and the appropriate model; subtracting an arbitrary geoid grid is not sufficient. See the geoid module and NOAA's geoid model documentation.

Paths, directions, and distances​

Geodesic​

A path that is locally straight on a surface, with zero geodesic curvature. Sufficiently short segments minimize surface distance, but an extended geodesic need not be the globally shortest route between its endpoints. A shortest-path geodesic solver chooses a minimizing segment; this distinction matters near antipodal points and when continuing a path beyond an endpoint.

On a sphere, geodesics are great circles. On an ellipsoid, a general geodesic is not a planar circle. Its bearing usually changes along the route. See ellipsoid geodesics and geodesic paths.

Great circle, small circle, and arc​

A great circle is the intersection of a sphere with a plane through its center. A small circle comes from an intersecting plane that does not pass through the center. An arc is a portion of a curve; the word alone does not specify whether it is a geodesic, a small-circle arc, or another path.

Two distinct, non-antipodal points on a sphere determine a shorter minor arc and a longer major arc on one great circle. For central angle θ in radians, the minor-arc length is R θ, with 0 < θ < π. Coincident or antipodal endpoints require additional choices when a route, rather than just a distance, is needed.

Rhumb line (loxodrome)​

A surface path of constant azimuth relative to local true north: it crosses meridians at a constant angle. A rhumb line generally differs from a shortest geodesic. On a normal Mercator map it appears as a straight line; an east–west rhumb follows a parallel, which is not generally a geodesic.

“Constant bearing” here means true-north bearing, not a constant uncorrected magnetic-compass reading. See ellipsoid rhumb lines.

Chord​

The straight three-dimensional segment joining two points. Between distinct points on a convex globe surface, its interior lies inside the globe rather than on the surface. On a sphere with central angle θ, chord length is 2R sin(θ/2), while the minor great-circle arc length is R θ.

For endpoints 90° apart, the arc is about 1.571 R and the chord 1.414 R. Linearly interpolating their ECEF coordinates follows the chord. Normalizing those interpolated positions onto a sphere follows the minor great-circle arc for non-antipodal endpoints, but generally not at constant speed. Spherical linear interpolation (slerp) provides constant angular speed along that arc.

Bearing, azimuth, and direct/inverse problems​

Azimuth is a direction angle in a specified local reference, commonly clockwise from true north. Bearing is often used for the same concept, but conventions differ. Grid north, geodetic true north, and magnetic north are not interchangeable. Grid convergence measures the angular relationship between grid north and true north, with sign depending on the convention.

The direct geodesic problem finds an endpoint from a starting point, initial azimuth, and distance. The inverse geodesic problem finds distance and azimuths from two endpoints. This use of “inverse” is distinct from inverse map projection.

Distance or lineWhere it is measuredTypical use
Geodesic distanceAlong the reference surfaceShortest surface routes
Rhumb distanceAlong a constant-azimuth surface pathFixed-heading routes
Chord distanceAlong a straight segment in 3DCartesian geometry and line-of-sight calculations
Projected distanceIn planar map coordinatesMap geometry; ground distance requires accounting for projection scale
Screen distanceIn display pixelsInteraction, rendering tolerances, and labels

Projections and distortion​

A map projection assigns planar coordinates to locations on a curved reference surface. A forward projection maps geographic to planar coordinates; an inverse projection attempts the reverse. Inverses can be undefined, non-unique, or restricted to a particular chart.

TermMeaning
ConformalPreserves local angles and infinitesimal shape at regular points. It does not preserve general areas or finite shapes.
Equal-area / equivalentPreserves areas up to a constant scale factor. It does not imply preservation of angles or lengths.
EquidistantPreserves specified distances, for example from a center or along selected lines. No flat map preserves every pairwise distance on a spherical region.
AzimuthalIn the normal construction, positions are arranged by distance and direction from a chosen center. The radial function determines the distortion property.
Compromise projectionBalances distortion without exactly preserving a single property everywhere; Robinson is an example.
Scale factorLocal projected length divided by the corresponding reference-surface length. It can vary with position and direction.
Standard parallelA selected parallel with the projection's prescribed true-scale condition. It is not necessarily the center of the map.
Central meridianThe longitude used as the projection's reference meridian, often its symmetry axis.
False easting / false northingConstant offsets added to projected coordinates, often to avoid negative values. They change coordinate values without changing projection distortion.
AspectOrientation of a projection relative to the globe: normal, transverse, or oblique. It differs from rotating the finished image in its plane.
GraticuleThe displayed network of meridians and parallels. It is not necessarily the computational mesh.
JacobianThe matrix of local derivatives of a coordinate mapping. It describes local scale and deformation at regular points.
Tissot indicatrixThe infinitesimal ellipse obtained by projecting a small circle on the surface; its axes illustrate local directional scale.
SingularityA location where the mapping or its inverse ceases to be regular, such as a collapsed pole or an infinite projected coordinate.

A CRS conversion changes coordinate representations without changing the underlying datum; a transformation changes the datum or reference frame and can involve empirical parameters, grids, and epochs. A projection formula alone is not a complete datum transformation. See the projection guide.

Charts, cuts, and map layouts​

TermMeaning in math.gl's map documentation
Chart / regionA geographic domain with its own projection and planar placement. This is an implementation chart, not necessarily a globally invertible mathematical coordinate chart.
Domain / footprintThe geographic area a chart represents. A finite projected coordinate does not by itself establish domain membership.
Spherical capThe part of a sphere within a given angular distance of a center. Its boundary is usually a small circle; a hemispherical cap has a great-circle boundary.
HorizonA visibility boundary for a specified viewpoint or projection. It is not always a hemisphere boundary.
ClippingIntersecting geometry with a domain and retaining the inside pieces. Correct clipping can add boundary vertices and split polygons.
SeamA boundary between charts, classified as a join or cut according to their placed boundary images.
JoinA seam where neighboring chart images meet continuously. A join need not be drawn as an outline.
Cut / interruptionA seam opened in the planar layout. Geometry crossing it must be split rather than connected across the gap.
Segmented mapA map constructed from multiple charts. Segmentation alone does not imply visible cuts or disconnected pieces.
Connected / disjointWhether pieces remain connected through their retained joins. A connected star map can still contain open cuts.
LobeA geographic section of an interrupted map, often with its own central meridian.
InsetA separately placed regional map, sometimes with a different scale or projection.
Polyhedral netA planar arrangement of projected polyhedron faces, joined along selected edges.
HingeAn edge and rotation axis relating adjacent faces during rigid unfolding. A continuous chart join does not automatically define a hinge.
Myriahedral mapA map built from many small faces, with cuts and retained adjacencies determining the unfolding layout.

Longitude wrapping changes a coordinate's numeric representative, for example 181° to −179°. It does not split a line or polygon at a cut. Unwrapping chooses continuous longitude representatives along a sequence; it also does not remove geometric seams. See discontinuous maps and the MapEngine reference.

Sampling, meshes, and tiles​

TermMeaning
Tessellation / triangulationReplacing a surface or polygon with simpler pieces, often triangles. Triangles must respect cuts and holes.
Subdivision / densificationAdding vertices to approximate a curved path or surface more closely. The interpolation model determines where those vertices lie.
Adaptive samplingRefining where an error estimate exceeds a tolerance, rather than using a fixed number of segments everywhere.
ToleranceAn allowed numerical or approximation error in stated units. Angular, projected-coordinate, and pixel tolerances are different quantities.
Accuracy / precisionAccuracy describes agreement with a reference or true value; precision describes repeatability or numerical detail. More decimal places do not guarantee better positional accuracy.
ResolutionSampling detail, such as grid spacing or pixels per map unit. It does not by itself measure positional accuracy.
Level of detail (LOD) / zoom levelA dataset- or renderer-specific choice of sampling scale. Separate insets can require different tile levels in one viewport.
Tile matrixA defined arrangement of tiles at a resolution, with an origin, bounds, tile dimensions, and axis conventions.
DGGSA discrete global grid system partitioning the globe into indexed cells. Its cell hierarchy and indexing are distinct from a planar projection or unfolding.

References and further reading​

These definitions describe mathematical and geodetic concepts, independently of any one implementation's function names.

  • John P. Snyder and Philip M. Voxland: An Album of Map Projections, for projection constructions, terminology, and distortion properties.
  • NASA GISS: G.Projector's projection reference, for named families and their properties.
