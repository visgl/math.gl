Solar-system bodies

🧪 Experimental — The @math.gl/earth/celestial subpath and all its exported functions, classes, constants, and types are experimental.

Rotation models, radii, gravitational parameters and approximate positions of the Sun, the planets and the Moon. The functions are pure. They include no ephemeris file, no kernel and no download. Each function takes a JulianDate in the TT scale.

import {
  JulianDate,
  getApproximatePlanetPosition,
  getBodyFixedFrame,
  getBodySubsolarPoint
} from '@math.gl/earth/celestial';

const tt = JulianDate.fromUnixMilliseconds(Date.now(), ttMinusUtc, 'TT');

// ICRF to the body-fixed frame of Mars, with its rate per second.
const frame = getBodyFixedFrame('mars', tt);

// The Sun as seen from Mars: the negative of the heliocentric position of Mars.
const [x, y, z] = getApproximatePlanetPosition('mars', tt);
const subsolar = getBodySubsolarPoint('mars', tt, [-x, -y, -z]);

Bodies​

SolarSystemBody is 'sun', 'mercury', 'venus', 'earth', 'moon', 'mars', 'jupiter', 'saturn', 'uranus' or 'neptune'. SOLAR_SYSTEM_BODIES lists them.

🧪 getBodyRadii(body)​

Returns {equatorialRadius, polarRadius} in metres. The values are those of the IAU WGCCRE 2015 report (Archinal et al. 2018, Tables 4 and 5) as NAIF pck00011.tpc gives them. The Earth values (6378136.6 m and 6356751.9 m) are the IAU values, not WGS84.

🧪 getBodyGravitationalParameter(body)​

Returns GM in m³/s², without the satellites of the body. The source is NAIF gm_de440.tpc: the DE440 values (Park et al. 2021) for the Sun, Mercury, Venus, the Earth and the Moon, and the JPL satellite ephemeris solutions for Mars to Neptune.

Rotation​

🧪 getBodyRotationElements(body, tt)​

Returns {poleRightAscension, poleDeclination, primeMeridian} in degrees: α0 and δ0 of the north pole in the ICRF, and the angle W of the prime meridian. α0 and W are in [0, 360). The models are the IAU WGCCRE 2015 models, with the periodic terms of Mercury, the Moon, Mars, Jupiter and Neptune.

🧪 getBodyFixedFrame(body, tt)​

Returns a CelestialFrameTransform from 'ICRF' to 'IAU_<BODY>' (for example 'IAU_MARS'). The rotation is Rz(W) Rx(90° − δ0) Rz(90° + α0), where each factor rotates the frame. The rate includes the derivatives of α0, δ0 and W, so transformState gives body-fixed velocities.

The supported dates are 1900-01-01T12:00 TT to 2100-01-01T12:00 TT. Other dates throw RangeError.

Limits:

  • The IAU Earth model is linear. It has no nutation and no polar motion, and it uses TT in place of UT1. Its error in W is of the order of 0.1 degree. Use an Earth orientation model for precise work on the Earth.
  • The IAU Moon model approximates the mean Earth/polar axis frame. It is not the principal axis frame of a lunar ephemeris.
  • The IAU models use TDB. The functions use TT in place of TDB; the two differ by less than 2 ms. At the same physical instant, that moves W by up to about 1e-5 degrees for Jupiter.

Approximate planet positions​

🧪 getApproximatePlanetPosition(planet, tt, options?)​

Returns the heliocentric position of a planet in au, from the Keplerian elements of E. M. Standish and J. G. Williams (JPL, "Approximate Positions of the Planets"). planet is 'mercury', 'venus', 'earth-moon-barycenter', 'mars', 'jupiter', 'saturn', 'uranus' or 'neptune'. The Earth is the Earth-Moon barycenter. options.frame is 'equatorial' (ICRF axes, the default) or 'ecliptic' (mean ecliptic and equinox of J2000).

The function uses Table 1 from 1800 to 2050, and Tables 2a and 2b from 3000 BC to 3000 AD outside that interval. At 2050-01-01T12:00 TT the position thus changes by a small step. Other dates throw RangeError. The position is geometric: it has no light time and no aberration.

JPL states these nominal errors of Table 1: longitude and latitude in arcseconds, distance in 1000 km. The measured column is the largest heliocentric error against DE440 from 1850 to 2050, in steps of 97.3 days. JPL calls its figures nominal, and the measured errors are larger for some planets. The tests store DE440 positions every 1000 days and use gates a little above the measured values.

For the Moon, or for better accuracy, supply positions from an ephemeris of your choice.

Lighting​

🧪 getBodySunDirection(body, tt, sunPosition)​

Returns the unit vector to the Sun in the body-fixed frame. sunPosition is the vector from the center of the body to the Sun in ICRF axes, in any unit, from any ephemeris.

🧪 getBodySubsolarPoint(body, tt, sunPosition)​

Returns the planetocentric {longitude, latitude} of the subsolar point in degrees, longitude east positive in (−180, 180]. The terminator is the great circle 90 degrees from this point, without refraction.

🧪 Experimental atmospheres​

These rendering presets combine published reference conditions with derived optics and assumed aerosol properties. Pressure, temperature and gas fractions set the base number density. The RGB Rayleigh coefficients follow from those inputs and the gas cross sections. Treat the Mars per-channel dust albedo and asymmetry and the Venus upper-haze optical depth, scale height, asymmetry and albedo as rendering assumptions. Wolff and Wilquet supply observational context. The shell cutoff and Venus cloud color are also model choices.

🧪 getBodyAtmosphere(body)​

Returns the single-scattering atmosphere parameters of a body, or null for the Sun, Mercury and the Moon. Mercury and the Moon have only a surface-bounded exosphere. BODIES_WITH_ATMOSPHERE lists the bodies that have parameters: Venus, the Earth, Mars and the four giant planets.

import {getBodyAtmosphere} from '@math.gl/earth/celestial';

const mars = getBodyAtmosphere('mars');
// Vertical Mie optical depth at 550 nm: 0.5.
const tau = mars.mieExtinction[1] * mars.mieScaleHeight;

The model is a spherical shell from baseRadius to topRadius (metres) with two exponential profiles: gas (Rayleigh) and aerosols (Mie). Each coefficient is the value at the base, in m⁻¹, for the wavelengths ATMOSPHERE_WAVELENGTHS (680, 550 and 440 nm). At height h above the base, the coefficient is the base value times exp(−h / H). Use the phase and Chapman functions of @math.gl/earth/sun (getRayleighPhaseFunction, getHenyeyGreensteinPhaseFunction, getChapmanGrazingIncidenceFunction) with these parameters.

The Earth has the demo constants of Bruneton (2017), which many renderers use. Its Rayleigh coefficient at 550 nm is about 18 % above the value of dry air at 15 °C and 101325 Pa. For the other bodies, the Rayleigh coefficient is the number density p / (k T) times the cross-section of getRayleighCrossSection.

Limits:

  • The values are typical. They are not a model of seasons, weather or dust storms.
  • Ozone absorption (Earth) and methane absorption (Uranus, Neptune) are not modeled. Methane is also left out of the Rayleigh sum.
  • The tops of the shells are model choices, at about 7 to 8 gas scale heights.
  • The cloud-deck albedo of Venus is a visual approximation near the Bond albedo 0.77, not a measured spectrum.

🧪 getRayleighCrossSection(gas, wavelength)​

Returns the Rayleigh cross-section of one molecule in m²: σ = 24 π³ / (λ⁴ Nₛ²) ((nₛ² − 1) / (nₛ² + 2))² F (Bodhaine et al. 1999, eq. 2). gas is 'air', 'carbon-dioxide', 'hydrogen' or 'helium'. wavelength is in metres, from 0.4e−6 to 0.8e−6. The refractivities are those of Ciddor (1996), Bideau-Mehu et al. (1973), Peck & Huang (1977) and Mansfield & Peck (1969). The King factor F of air is that of Bates (1984), mixed as Bodhaine et al. (1999) mix it. F is 1.15 for CO2 and 1 for H2 and He. For air at 550 nm the result is 4.51e−31 m².

Qualification​

modules/earth/scripts/celestial/generate-body-reference.py writes the stored controls. It uses CSPICE N0067 (SpiceyPy 8.4.0) with NAIF pck00011.tpc and de440s.bsp, pinned by SHA-256, and an independent Python evaluation of the JPL element tables, parsed from the JPL table text. It does not run the TypeScript code. Ordinary CI uses only the stored controls.

The rotation, frame and subsolar gates state agreement with CSPICE at the same numerical ephemeris-time argument. The generator gives CSPICE the TT seconds from J2000.0 as TDB seconds, so the controls test the rotation models, not the TT-to-TDB conversion. At the same physical instant the TT−TDB difference (less than 2 ms) moves W by up to about 1e-5 degrees for Jupiter. A same-instant accuracy claim needs a TT-to-TDB conversion and its own controls.
